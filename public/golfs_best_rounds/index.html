Prologue  ·  Royal Birkdale, July 2026

Three 62s in two days. Are these the best rounds in a major ever?

Not everyone can agree. So we pulled every scorecard from every major championship at Royal Birkdale and Augusta National: events and rounds, going back to 1983. Let's see how far we get with the question.

Spoiler

We aren't going to settle it definitively. We're not even looking at every major venue. In an effort to interpret this question, we're taking a look at just two courses with deep-enough history. What you'll leave with is four alternate ways of answering the question, and a much sharper idea of what that question could mean.

Start
01 — the argument

There are two obvious camps

Within an hour of the third 62, netizens had settled into two broad positions. Both have points to make with merit.

Camp one · count the strokes

Par is a committee's opinion. Sixty-two strokes is a fact.

Measuring against par doesn't make the number more objective — it buries one judgement inside another. Somebody sat down and decided that hole #13 was a par-4.

Camp two · count against something

Then hand a major to a par 70 with four driveable 4s and tell me the raw total is just as meaningful.

Strokes only carry meaning against what the course was asking for. Birkdale is a par 70 — that 62 is eight under. A 63 at Augusta is nine under, and nobody's shouting about it.

Here's the problem with picking a side: you would need to be far more precise in defining a "best round?" — best relative to what, corrected for what? Change your answer to that, and the leaderboard changes with it.

+Rabbit hole — who gets to be on this board?

A board of ten needs a pool of candidates, and choosing that pool is already a judgement call. Rank by strokes and pick the top 50, and you have quietly guaranteed that low-par courses dominate every later re-ranking — the shuffle would be rigged before it started.

So the candidate pool is the union: the top 50 rounds under every lens we're going to use, pooled together. A round earns its place if any reasonable definition of "great" rates it highly. rounds qualify; the board only ever shows you ten of them at a time.

This costs us something worth naming: rounds that are merely very good (as opposed to great) under all four lenses never appear, while rounds that are spectacular under one lens and mediocre under the rest do. That is a skew — a somewhat defensible choice, but not a neutral one. That said, the final interactive leaderboard in the Practice Ground simultaneously re-ranks all rounds.

Par is a hypothesis. So let's score it.

Par is a claim: this is what this golf course asks of a very good player. Every championship tests that claim, because the field's average round tells us what the course actually asked for. Call that number effective par.

Below, the dashed lines are what the committees wrote down. The solid lines are what the golf courses collected.

And look where the solid lines sit: nearly always above the dashed ones. Par is not a typical score at a major championship. It is closer to a very good one.

One honest wrinkle before anyone else spots it: Augusta's solid line swings wider than Birkdale's. That isn't Augusta being more volatile — it's Augusta having championships to Birkdale's , and therefore far more chances to produce an extreme year. Hold that thought; we'll come back to how volatility is actually measured in Chapter 3.

Why the two lines come unstuck

Two different mechanisms, one at each course — and neither committee did anything unreasonable.

Royal Birkdale · par The number changed: 71 in 1983, 70 from 1991 onwards.
Augusta National · yardage The ground changed: par 72 throughout, while the course grew ~650 yards. Dashed = not published.

In 1983 the 13th at Birkdale was a 505-yard par 5. By 1991 it was a 475-yard par 4 — thirty yards shorter, and a stroke dearer. The whole course shrank slightly over the same stretch, 6,968 yards down to 6,940, while its par fell from 71 to 70. Nothing about the golf got easier. The bookkeeping simply changed.

Augusta went the other way: it kept the number and moved the golf course, adding 650 yards without ever admitting it on the card. One changed the number. The other changed the ground. Either way, printed par and played difficulty came unstuck.

Don't overstate it

The conclusion isn't that par is meaningless. On the contrary, par is a good local convention. It's an estimate and a common yardstick for that week. It isn't a prediction for the field.

What par can't do is travel. Treating it as gospel across courses, tours, continents and decades asks a local instrument to do global work — which is exactly what the phrase "the lowest round in major championship history" demands of it.

+Rabbit hole — didn't golf already solve this with course rating and slope?

"Par doesn't measure difficulty" is not a new discovery. Handicapping bodies worked it out decades ago and built the replacement: course rating — the score an expert player is expected to shoot — and slope, which describes how much faster the course gets harder for everyone else. A course can read par 70 and be rated 74.5, and that gap is precisely the one we just drew.

Two reasons it can't do our job. First, ratings describe a course's ordinary configuration; the R&A and Augusta arrive and reset tees, pins, rough and green speeds into a setup no rating panel ever measured. Second and more fundamentally, a rating is a statement about architecture — yardage, hazards, green complexity, the things that are true on a calm Tuesday in March. It is deliberately blind to the Friday gale, which is the single biggest thing that ever happened to scoring at Royal Birkdale.

Effective par is the cruder instrument, and for now it's the one we want: it doesn't care why the course was hard, only that it was. In Chapter 3 that stops being a virtue and becomes the problem.

As we've already seen, stated par and what the field shoots on average are two different things. Par corrects for what the architects intended. It cannot correct for what the golf course was doing on one particular afternoon — and at one of our two venues, that turns out to be most of the story.

Chapter 2 — Two kinds of hard  →
Overlook · detour

Was Augusta ever really Tiger-proofed?

Between 1999 and 2006 Augusta National added roughly 500 yards, pushed tees back into the trees and grew a second cut of rough — a response, everyone agreed, to one player in particular. The phrase stuck: Tiger-proofing. If it worked, it should show up in the scores.

It doesn't. The field's average round today, on yards, sits under a stroke over par. In 1989, on 6,905 yards, it was closer to two and a half. The two gentlest Masters in the entire dataset, , were both played on the long version of the golf course.

The honest reading is that this was never a controlled experiment. Across the same four decades the ball and the driver changed beyond recognition, agronomy turned fairways into runways, and the field arriving in April got deeper every year. Augusta added 650 yards and roughly held its ground. That may be exactly what winning looks like — left at 6,905 yards, the same course might be surrendering 64s every afternoon.

Unresolved, and we're leaving it that way. Separating architecture from equipment from field strength needs data this project doesn't have: driving distances, green speeds, a field-strength index by year. What we can say is narrow and true — the lengthening did not raise scores. Why it didn't is a bigger question than two golf courses can answer.

One more thing, and it pokes a hole in something we all assume

Every golf fan carries a mental card for a famous course. Birkdale? Brutal. Augusta? Gettable if you're on. We talk about courses as though they have a settled character — and the numbers above encouraged that, because an average is exactly the sort of thing that sounds like a personality.

So what have we actually learned?

An average flatters a golf course with a personality it may not have. "Royal Birkdale plays strokes harder than Augusta National" is true, and it hides the thing that matters: what you get when you turn up at Birkdale is far more up in the air — literally and figuratively — than what you get at Augusta.

And with only visits on record, that in 2008 can't be waved away as a freak. One week in is the typical experience for someone who visits times. Augusta, over attempts, has never once produced a year that hard.

Something moved everyone at once in 2008 and moved them all back in 2017. It wasn't the architecture — the holes barely changed. Whatever it was is bigger than the difference we set out to measure, and it isn't on the scorecard at all.

+Rabbit hole — why we're not comparing the two spreads yet

The obvious next move is to compare how spread out each course's rounds are, and the two numbers look almost identical: a standard deviation of at Birkdale against at Augusta. Case closed, apparently — same variability, different average.

That comparison is a trap, and it's worth knowing why before Chapter 3 walks into it. A single standard deviation pools together two completely different sources of spread: players differing from each other on the same day, and days differing from each other. Two courses can post the same total spread while being built from wildly different mixtures of the two — and the mixture, not the total, is what actually explains the 2008-versus-2017 gap above.

So we’re deliberately not reading anything into versus . The number is real; it just answers a question nobody asked. Prising those two ingredients apart is the whole job of the next chapter.

No bell this chapter

Nothing here moves the leaderboard. Everything in this chapter was about golf courses; the board ranks golf rounds, and to-par doesn't know what year it is. An interesting fact and a relevant fact are different things — worth noticing, since plenty of what follows is one without being the other.

But hold two of the Birkdale 62s in your head — Branden Grace's in 2017, Ryan Fox's in 2026. How do the two compare?

What actually counts as a good score?

Every amateur golfer carries the same instinct: par is the standard, and a round of level par means you played to it. Which is a reasonable thing to believe, and at a major championship it is completely wrong.

%
of all Masters rounds are beaten by a score of level par
%
of all Birkdale Open rounds are beaten by a score of level par

Walk into the scoring tent at Royal Birkdale having shot level par and you have just beaten % of the best golfers in the world that day. At Augusta you've beaten %. Par isn't the standard. Par is a good day.

It reads oddly because of where the number comes from. Par is set by what a hole should yield to an expert playing it well — two putts and a sensible approach — and then the championship goes and asks that expert to do it four days running, on greens rolled to the edge of legality, with pins tucked behind bunkers and a trophy at stake. The number describes the architecture. It was never a forecast of the tournament.

Which is why the broadcast convention of treating red numbers as ordinary is quietly misleading. Red numbers are the exception. On a normal day at a major, most of the field is going backwards.

Act II · Chapter 3  ·  the invisible player

Ask a player what beat them on a particularly rough day at Birkdale and they'll probably mention the wind

Every links championship produces the same post-round shrug — we just caught it at the wrong time — and every one of us has nodded along without ever seeing it quantified.

Ideally we'd pair every scorecard with wind speed and rainfall for that afternoon. We don't have that. But there turns out to be more hiding in the scores themselves than you'd expect — enough to put a number on it.

In addition to looking at player rounds, we can also look at days. Group every round by the day it was played, take each day's field average, and watch how much those averages move. One golfer can have a shocker. An entire field can't, unless something moved them all at once — and whatever does that isn't the golfer.

Every score contains two ingredients: how the player did compared to everyone else out there that day, and what sort of day it was. The players are right. The conditions aren't an excuse or a fabrication — they're a measurable tax — and at a links course it is one of the largest single factors in the scoring, larger than anything we corrected for in the first two chapters put together.

Why we call it weather+

Strictly, we've measured "everything that moved a whole field at once." Mostly that's wind and rain — but it also covers the pin positions, the tee placements, and, across the years, redesigns and rough height. Hence the plus.

We can still put a floor under the weather part, because the two kinds of cause run on different clocks. A golf course doesn't get redesigned between Thursday and Friday. So look only at how much a field average moves inside a single championship week, where the architecture is nailed down and nothing is left but the sky and that morning's setup.

How much a day's field average swings strokes
inside one championship week — architecture frozen across all days and all years

Same holes, same rough, same yardage, nothing touched — and a day at Royal Birkdale still swings by strokes. Versus Augusta, at strokes.

Treat that as a floor. Not an estimate of what the weather costs you, and certainly not a ceiling. The between-years figure clearly has multiple causes (hole redesigns, rough heights, tee positions and more), but the years also differed in wind, and, barring richer data, we have no way to pull those apart.

Possibly, therefore, a stroke and a half of Royal Birkdale's character is the weather. The real number is likely bigger than ±, and we can't say by exactly how much.

So what does this buy us?

A fairer question. Instead of asking how did this round compare to par, we can now ask how did this round compare to what everyone else managed that same day — on the same course, same setup, off the same pins.

It is the closest thing to a level playing field this data can offer. It also costs us something, and the price becomes obvious the moment we apply it.

+Rabbit hole — the arithmetic behind those two percentages

Group every round by its day. Within each day, measure how far players sit from their own day's average: that spread is strokes at Birkdale and at Augusta — near enough identical, because golfers are golfers. Then measure how far the day averages sit from each other: at Birkdale against at Augusta. Nearly double.

To express one as a share of the total, the two have to be squared first — shares are computed on variance, not on standard deviations, because variances add and standard deviations don't:

day's share = between² ÷ (between² + within²)

Which gives % at Royal Birkdale and % at Augusta National. Statisticians call this the intra-class correlation, because it also happens to equal the expected correlation between two random players' scores on the same day — they're correlated precisely because they share a day. "The day's share of the variance" is the more useful reading here.

One caution we'll collect on later: both figures are themselves estimates, and Birkdale's rests on just days. That bill never goes away — and the Practice Ground charges it openly: slice the data thin enough there and the marks turn hollow to tell you so.

Overlook · detour

The weather got into the record books too

Our archive of the 1983 Open contains players. The field that week was 151. Twenty-nine golfers are simply missing.

They are not missing at random, which is the problem. Historical archives thin out from the bottom: the players who vanish are the ones who missed the cut — the ones having the worst week. Every round we're missing from 1983 is a bad one.

So the early championships in this dataset look slightly kinder than they actually were, and the effect lands hardest exactly where we're least able to absorb it: on Thursday and Friday, at the course with only visits on record.

We can't fix it — the rounds were never digitised. We can size it. If those 29 golfers averaged what the rest of the field's missed-cut players averaged, 1983's opening two days were perhaps a quarter of a stroke harder than we're showing.

Left standing, and disclosed. A quarter of a stroke is small next to the -stroke weather range we've just measured — but it is not zero, and it always points the same way. Every number in this piece that leans on the 1983 Open is a touch too flattering.
